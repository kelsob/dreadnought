Dreadnought
1. High Concept
A first-person survival/operations game where the player inhabits the cockpit of a gigantic, heavily armed combat dreadnought.
The player does not operate the machine through conventional videogame controls. They physically operate the machine through its cockpit systems.
The central fantasy is being one human trapped inside an enormous, ancient, complicated war machine.
The player is not an omniscient commander looking at a HUD and issuing abstract commands. They are the pilot/operator sitting in a seat, looking around the cockpit, physically manipulating controls, monitoring systems, and trying to keep the machine functioning.
The machine should feel extremely large, heavy, slow, powerful, and difficult to operate.
The cockpit is therefore a major part of the game rather than simply a visual framing device.
________________


2. Perspective
The game is played entirely from the first-person perspective of the pilot.
The player's physical position inside the cockpit is fixed.
The player cannot freely walk around the cockpit.
The player's "body" remains seated in the pilot's position, while their head can turn within a limited range.
Looking around should feel like turning a human head while seated inside a machine, rather than controlling a free-floating camera.
The player should be able to look:
* Left
* Right
* Up
* Down
but within believable physical limits.
The cockpit surrounds the player and contains the physical controls required to operate the dreadnought.
________________


3. The Dreadnought
The dreadnought is a massive combat machine that moves on two enormous mechanical legs.
It is closer to a walking industrial fortress than a conventional vehicle.
Its scale should be emphasized constantly.
The machine is:
* Extremely large
* Heavy
* Mechanically complex
* Slow to respond
* Powerful
* Difficult to operate
* Capable of carrying substantial weaponry and equipment
The player should always feel that there is an enormous amount of machinery between their actions and the outside world.
A lever moving inside the cockpit may ultimately cause a gigantic leg outside the machine to move hundreds of tonnes of machinery.
________________


4. Core Control Philosophy
The most important design principle is:
The player does not tell the machine what to do. The player operates the machine.
There should be as little abstraction as possible.
For locomotion in particular, there is no simple:
* "Move forward"
* "Move backward"
* "Turn left"
* "Turn right"
input.
Instead, the player manually controls the two legs.
Every individual step is commanded through a physical control.
The intention is for movement to feel more like operating a complicated mechanical machine than controlling a character.
________________


5. Leg Controls
The dreadnought has two massive primary controls:
* Left leg control
* Right leg control
These are enormous physical levers located within reach of the pilot.
Each lever moves along a forward/backward axis.
Conceptually, each lever has three states:
FORWARD
   |
NEUTRAL
   |
BACKWARD


The player can manipulate each leg independently.
The levers are not conventional throttles.
They represent the position/command of the corresponding leg.
________________


6. Standing / Neutral
When the game begins, both legs are in their neutral stationary positions.
LEFT  = NEUTRAL
RIGHT = NEUTRAL


The dreadnought is stationary.
Neutral represents a leg being in its stable planted position relative to the body of the machine.
The player can remain stationary indefinitely with both legs neutral.
There is no need for a conventional "stop" button.
________________


7. Forward Locomotion
Forward movement is manually constructed one step at a time.
To begin moving forward, the player moves the left leg control forward.
The left leg takes a forward step.
The right leg remains neutral.
The player then moves the right leg control forward.
The right leg takes a forward step, while the left leg returns to its neutral position.
The player then repeats the process.
Conceptually:
START


L = NEUTRAL
R = NEUTRAL


L = FORWARD
R = NEUTRAL


L = NEUTRAL
R = FORWARD


L = FORWARD
R = NEUTRAL


L = NEUTRAL
R = FORWARD


Forward movement therefore consists of an alternating sequence of individual leg commands.
There is no automatic walking cycle.
The player must continue operating the controls to continue walking.
The rhythm of the controls should become something the player physically learns.
________________


8. Backward Locomotion
Backward movement uses exactly the same fundamental system.
Instead of moving each leg forward, the player moves each leg backward.
Conceptually:
START


L = NEUTRAL
R = NEUTRAL


L = BACKWARD
R = NEUTRAL


L = NEUTRAL
R = BACKWARD


L = BACKWARD
R = NEUTRAL


L = NEUTRAL
R = BACKWARD


Again, every step is individually commanded.
________________


9. Automatic Leg Reset
The controls have an important relationship.
When the player commands the currently neutral leg in the same direction as the previous step, the other leg returns to neutral.
For example:
L = FORWARD
R = NEUTRAL


The player then commands:
R = FORWARD


The previous left-leg position is reset to neutral:
L = NEUTRAL
R = FORWARD


This creates the alternating gait.
The player is therefore constantly switching between the two controls.
The controls themselves should physically reflect this relationship.
The player should be able to feel and see the controls moving between their forward, neutral, and backward positions.
________________


10. Turning
Turning uses the exact same two controls.
There is no requirement for lateral controls.
Instead, the player causes rotation by commanding the legs in opposite directions.
For example, one direction of rotation is:
LEFT  = FORWARD
RIGHT = BACKWARD


The opposing direction is:
RIGHT = FORWARD
LEFT  = BACKWARD


The difference in movement between the two legs causes the dreadnought's body to pivot.
A simplified right-turn sequence can therefore be:
LEFT FORWARD
RIGHT BACKWARD
LEFT FORWARD
RIGHT BACKWARD
...


A left-turn sequence is the reverse:
RIGHT FORWARD
LEFT BACKWARD
RIGHT FORWARD
LEFT BACKWARD
...


This allows the entire fundamental locomotion system to remain contained within the two leg controls.
The player is effectively steering the machine by deciding where each leg goes rather than operating a separate steering mechanism.
________________


11. Turning While Moving
Turning does not have to be restricted to stationary pivots.
Because the player controls each leg individually, they can create different movement patterns to alter the machine's trajectory.
The eventual system can support:
* Straight forward movement
* Straight backward movement
* Gradual directional changes
* Tight turns
* Stationary pivots
* Precise positioning
All of these emerge from the relationship between the two leg controls.
There should not need to be a separate abstract movement system layered over the leg controls.
________________


12. Step Size
The eventual locomotion system should allow the player to control the magnitude of an individual step.
The further the player moves a leg control from neutral, the greater the commanded movement of that leg.
A small movement of the control should produce a small step.
A large movement should produce a large step.
This allows the player to make precise adjustments rather than being restricted to fixed-size steps.
For example:
Small lever movement
→ small positional adjustment


Large lever movement
→ large stride


This is important because the dreadnought should not feel like it moves in discrete videogame-sized chunks.
The player should be able to carefully position the machine.
________________


13. Physicality of the Controls
The leg controls should feel enormous and mechanical.
They are not tiny joysticks.
They are substantial pieces of machinery connected conceptually to the gigantic legs of the dreadnought.
The player should physically manipulate them.
Eventually, the controls should be capable of communicating information through:
* Position
* Resistance
* Movement
* Mechanical feedback
* Sound
* Vibration
The player should be able to develop an understanding of the machine through the controls themselves.
The goal is for the cockpit to feel like a real machine rather than a collection of videogame buttons.
________________


14. Cockpit Philosophy
The cockpit should be heavily diegetic.
Important information should ideally be represented by physical objects, mechanical indicators, instruments, lights, gauges, displays, or other elements that plausibly exist inside the machine.
The cockpit is effectively the player's interface to the entire game.
There should be minimal reliance on conventional videogame HUD elements.
The player should look around the cockpit to operate things.
Controls should have physical locations.
Different systems should occupy different parts of the cockpit.
The player should eventually need to physically look toward and interact with different pieces of equipment.
________________


15. The Player's Relationship With the Machine
The player is not the machine.
The player is its operator.
This distinction is important.
The dreadnought should have its own physical limitations and mechanical behavior.
The player's job is to understand those limitations and operate within them.
The machine may eventually have problems involving:
* Mechanical damage
* System failures
* Power
* Repairs
* Weapons
* Environmental hazards
* Other operational complications
The player should deal with these problems from inside the cockpit rather than through abstract menus.
________________


16. Combat
The dreadnought is fundamentally a combat machine.
Its enormous size and heavy mechanical construction make it capable of carrying significant weapons.
Combat should preserve the same design philosophy as locomotion.
The player should eventually operate weapons through physical cockpit controls rather than simply selecting weapons from a HUD.
Weapons and combat systems should feel like machinery the player has to operate.
The player should have to manage the machine while simultaneously dealing with external threats.
The dreadnought's physical scale should make combat feel consequential.
A weapon firing should feel like an enormous mechanical event rather than an FPS weapon firing from the player's camera.
________________


17. Survival / Operations
The game is not intended to be purely about combat.
A major part of the experience is operating and maintaining the dreadnought.
The machine is enormous and complicated.
Keeping it functioning should eventually become a central part of gameplay.
The player may need to:
* Diagnose problems
* Operate machinery
* Repair damaged systems
* Manage resources
* Respond to failures
* Maintain weapons
* Maintain locomotion
* Deal with environmental conditions
* Make decisions about which systems to prioritize
The cockpit should therefore gradually become a familiar workspace that the player learns to operate.
________________


18. Atmosphere
The intended tone is industrial, oppressive, isolated, and mechanical.
The player should feel small compared with the machine they inhabit.
The dreadnought should not feel sleek or futuristic in the conventional science-fiction sense.
It should feel like a gigantic piece of machinery that requires human beings to operate it.
Controls should have weight.
Movement should have inertia.
Machines should make noise.
Systems should have physical consequences.
The cockpit should feel enclosed and somewhat claustrophobic despite the enormous scale of the machine outside.
________________


19. The Core Player Experience
The long-term goal is for the player to develop a physical understanding of their machine.
At the beginning, operating it should feel complicated.
Eventually, the player should develop an intuitive rhythm.
For locomotion, the player might initially think:
Left forward. Right forward. Left forward. Right forward.
Eventually it should become muscle memory.
The same principle should apply to the rest of the cockpit.
The player should gradually become familiar with:
* Where controls are
* What different sounds mean
* How the legs respond
* How quickly the machine turns
* How far a step will carry it
* How the machine behaves when damaged
* How to operate its weapons
* How to respond to failures
The fantasy is not simply piloting a giant robot.
It is learning how to operate a gigantic machine.
________________


20. Prototype Direction
The earliest prototype should establish the fundamental experience of being physically present inside the cockpit.
The initial focus is intentionally extremely small:
* First-person fixed cockpit position
* Restricted head movement
* Physical cockpit space
* Two physical leg-control levers
* Basic interaction with those controls
The eventual locomotion system described above is the long-term design target, but it does not need to exist in the earliest prototype.
The project should grow outward from the cockpit and its physical interaction model rather than beginning with conventional character movement and adding a cockpit around it.
